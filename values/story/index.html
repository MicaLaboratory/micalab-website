Why I wrote these values

Tom Mitchell

mica lab values

Academia is an odd place to work. It is problematic in so many ways, but I love the fizz in the air during Freshers' Week. The campus is full of young adults, dressed up and uncertain about where they're going but excited by new horizons, with the time and space to think about who they want to be. Universities are incredible environments for growth where both students and staff can follow ideas wherever they go, for the joy of just finding things out. That's rare and important. It's why I'm here. However, there are also lots of awful things about academia. Relentless obsessing over status, metrics, KPIs and league tables. A general fog of precarity and doom-mongering. Gruelling teaching workloads, pressures to publish and attain research funding. Outside my own environment, you find toxic spaces full of elitism and arrogance. You have to keep yourself in check to progress with integrity. Early career researchers finding their way are especially vulnerable: exploitation by academics with too much power and too little oversight is close to the default. It's easy to get ground down, hollowed out and burnt out. I know, because it happened to me.

In October 2024 I went mad,1 partly because I had been living and working in a way that did not align with my (unwritten) values. What came next was the most difficult two years of my life. I experienced a temporary period of profound impairment and medicalisation which has completely transformed my outlook on almost everything. I consider this period both a wake-up call and by far the most interesting thing about me. Along the way, I found refuge in stories of madness on Reddit, disability justice communities and posthuman literature. Posthumanism has taught me many things, but most importantly, that your actions express your moral framework, whether you acknowledge that you have one or not.

Of all the people that I have met and worked with in and out of academia, the virtue that I admire the most in others is integrity. Early in my career and still today, I have appreciated people's small acts of kindness and mentorship. I respect people's willingness to take the time to listen. I am impressed when people respectfully push back and say no to opportunities that conflict with their values. And I most admire those who ask difficult questions and stand up to power. Some of the people who have shown me what integrity looks like include Jane Mitchell, Sifu Wong Kiew Kit, Will Curtis, David Creasey, Imogen Fox, Jo Lansdowne, Clare Reddington, Erinma Ochu, Danielle Sinnett, Becs Andrews, Michael Brockhurst, David Corne, Alison Davies, Catherine Hobbs, Elena Marco, Verity McIntosh, Rob Arbon, Imogen Heap, Andrew McPherson, Adam Stark, Ben Gaster, Lisa May Thomas, Sophie Savage, Jess Thom, Matthew Pountney, Kris Halpin and Kat Hawkins.

I understand that being able to live by your values is a privilege, and that upholding them can sometimes mean making sacrifices. So I wrote down the values that guide how we work in the lab. They are written partially in response to my own and others' failings, as well as being inspired by the people named above.
